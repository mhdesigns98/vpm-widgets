When was the last time you drove through a toll booth in Richmond? Soon, they’ll be a thing of the past, at least as far as the RMTA (Richmond Metro Transit Authority) is concerned. The transition isn’t unexpected, beginning with the advent of Fastoll (now EZPass) in 1996, it’s coming to its natural conclusion in December 2026 when RMTA roads will be fully automated. These are the tollbooths that appear on the Downtown Expressway, Powhite Parkway, the Boulevard Bridge, and Forest Hill Avenue.

Established in 1966, RMTA toll collection attendants (TCA) have been a constant presence at the booths for 60 years. For many folks, interacting with TCAs has been part of their daily commute, a quick smile as they pass through, or an even longer exchange. It’s just one more area where casual and spontaneous human interaction is being phased out and automation is taking over.

As Maria J, the Deputy Director of Operations says, “We hate to see the people aspect leave, but the system that allowed that was not functioning. Parts were hard to come by or they were not available. It’s a move that a lot of agencies have made, but trying to sustain the legacy system was a challenge.”

Being a TCA was an all weather job, so even when the city was shut down, it was likely that a toll booth would be occupied — a beacon in the storm, someone with whom to commiserate or get directions. In light of this transition, please enjoy a few interviews conducted with a handful of the TCAs that remain. Their responses have been edited but are in their own words.

Tommy | 47 years on the job | 5 years as gate attendant, the rest of the time shift supervisor

What brought you to this line of work?

I needed a job, I was 19 and working one of the last full service gas stations in Richmond. After I lost that job, I needed another one and they called me in. I knew several people who worked here at the time, and I took it. I expected to work here til Philip Morris called or Virginia Power or whatever. But they never called. That or I missed it — one or the other. Been here ever since.

Currently being a shift supervisor is mostly monitoring screens, what did it look like before that?

When I came down here (Powhite Parkway), this was a complete toll plaza, you know, all the way across, both directions. We separated it in 2008.

We would supervise the toll collectors, kept them in change, making sure they had change out there so they could do their job. We were responsible for the money we had in here. And when I started, we would collect the vaults – which is where the money falls in. They throw it into a chute, which goes into a vault (and triggers the gate to let the person through).

If you want to get technical, we don’t really have toll collectors now. In ’95, they collected the money and pushed the button and at the end of the shift, they would have to account for all that money.

This is a lot better, you get more money for one. It’s a lot more secure. If they get robbed out there, they don't have much. In 1995, they could have had quite a bit of money.

Anything particularly rewarding or challenging about the job?

There's always something different. It was always something different. Always. You think you saw it all, but you didn't.

What was the hardest part of the job?

It’s an all-weather job. The heat didn't bother me too much, but the snow, the cold. When the toll plaza went all the way across we had reversible lanes that would go one way in the evening and one way in the morning. And we had traffic cones that split the highway. Twice a day you had to go out there and move them from a couple lanes over and you had to do it no matter what. Whether it was snowing or whatever. A lot of times, depending on how much it’s snowing, you just put them in the middle. But once it lightened up a little, you had to go back to normal.

The gate attendant didn't have a toll booth. They were out there watching the exact change gates to make sure drivers weren’t stuck. Gate attendants were actually outside walking around in the weather.

We didn't really rotate too much, If I was working 9 times out of 10, I got gates. And the rest of them got toll booths. Which was fine, I didn’t care. It meant I didn’t have to worry about a money trade. I didn’t have to worry about that because gate people don’t do that.

But that’s the only thing — the cold. That was the hardest.

Tommy didn’t have a lot of regulars because he was a gate attendant, but folks in the toll booth did:

That was a big part of it — the customer services part. That’s what keeps the people happy. Good people in the booth. If they’re pleasant, folks want to come back through, ‘the attendant always says something to me’, then they bring you stuff — like coffee.

That's what kept the people going was toll collectors. That's what separates this road from the rest of them.

Anything else you want to add:

This job wasn’t what I thought it was when I got it. Here at the main toll plazas, we had more people under 50 than we had over 50 at that time. I was 19 and I wasn't the youngest. There were a couple of guys that were younger than me, they were 18. We had great benefits here, too. I hate to say it, but it's not much of a real career move like it was when I started, We had, I want to say, 22 or 23 toll collectors at the Downtown Expressway when I started, and that was the smaller class.

The E-ZPass has alleviated all of that. The Expressway ended up over 50% EZPass users some years ago. And it doesn't back up at all, really, anymore. It keeps flowing, unless there's a screw up somewhere.

If you worked back in those days and went on hiatus and were suddenly dropped in here now, you just wouldn't recognize it. There's nothing going on out there (referring to Powhite Expressway where there are no more TCAs).

The four toll collectors that were in the booth at the DTE back in the day, they were steady all day long. We definitely went through some change back in those days, quite a bit.

MICHAEL | 20 years old | 2 years on the job | Toll attendant

What brought you to the job?

It’s isolated, but not entirely void of contact. You’re in a booth by yourself but you still get to talk to people coming through. It was like the perfect little middle ground and it seemed like the right fit for me. It’s not an easy job by any means. No job is. But I was just like, you know what? Seems like a chill change of pace. Just got out of fast food. I need to have a relaxing job next.

When I clock in, I don't really say anything. I usually pull up, go into the office, clock in, and go right to the booth. Even if I’m early, because I just feel like I should be working the moment I clock in. I’m not trying to stand around while things are going on, I go straight to the booth, and I usually just leave my coworkers alone and let them do their thing because they’re also in their own little worlds. That’s what the booth is like, just your own little pocket. So I leave them be.

What is the most challenging part of the job?

Like any customer service job, the customers. Maybe one out of every 10 or 15 causes a problem — but nothing outrageous. They can get upset over anything. The price — which hasn’t changed for two years. People think that one toll is equivalent to all tolls, so sometimes they come with complaints from another toll that I don’t have information about. Other times it’s E-ZPass and they want to know how they can get in with E-ZPass. I gotta let them know I’m not personally E-ZPass. You have to contact them. And then other times it’s just stuff they’re carrying with them throughout their day. So they already have an attitude. Not with me personally, just generally. It’s rare that they’re mad at me personally, and it’s not for reasons that I can think of that make sense. Like, the other day someone was upset because I was trying to help a customer and it was taking a little too long for their liking so they got mad with me. But it is what it is.

We used to count the cars that passed through our lanes, I liked knowing how many cars I got in an hour a day. It was fun thing to keep track of. I still remember my high score to this day. It was, like, 1,290 cars in one day.

Most surprising thing about the job:

I suppose it would be the things that you see on the road and how the customers interact with each other. Obviously, as a driver, you know, you get cut off a lot. Maybe you cut other people off, but seeing it from a third person point of view is something insane. You see people cut each other off and you think, oohhh, that could have been an accident, but it wasn’t. Or you see people speed through and you think, I hope that’s not how I look.

Favorite thing about the job:

Seeing the old cars. It’s surprising because I know people collect antique cars, but I didn’t know they actually drove them. When summer hits, that’s the best time.

What’s the strangest thing you’ve found in the basket?

A chicken bone, while on gate patrol, while I was searching the buckets and cleaning out the change.

What do you want people to know about this job?

You will not last if you cannot handle people.
